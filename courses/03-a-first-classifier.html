Part 1 · covers baselines and softmax regression

From features to ten probabilities

5 min live

Whatever features you hand it, the classifier tonight does the same three things. It computes one weighted sum of the features for each of the ten classes. It turns those ten sums into ten probabilities that add up to 1. It picks the largest. Before trusting any of that, print what a model scores when it ignores the image entirely.

LiveSoftmax regression in plain words3 min▶

The model holds one weight per feature per class. With 288 convolution features and 10 classes that is 2,880 weights, plus one bias per class. For an image, each class gets a score: multiply every feature by that class's weight and add them up. A large positive weight means "this feature is evidence for this class"; a negative one means evidence against.

Softmax turns the ten scores into probabilities: raise e to each score so they are all positive, then divide by their total so they sum to 1. The highest score always becomes the highest probability; softmax only changes how confident it sounds. Argmax picks the class. Training nudges the weights, step by step, to raise the probability of the right class on every labelled image: that is gradient descent. How the nudges are computed is backpropagation, covered in Deep Learning session 2, and the loop that repeats them is Deep Learning session 3. Tonight treats the training as a black box with known inputs and outputs.

The bench version is deliberately plain: features standardised, weights starting at zero, 150 full-batch gradient steps, learning rate 0.5, a small L2 penalty of 0.001. Plain is the point. When the classifier stays fixed, any difference in accuracy comes from the features or the labels, not from tuning.

Self-studyWhy a majority baseline comes first4 min▶

A majority baseline answers every image with the most common class in the training labels. On a balanced test set like Fashion-MNIST's, with 1,000 images per class, it scores about 10%, one class in ten. The bench's 300 random labels happen to hold 43 Shirts, more than any other class, so the bench baseline always says Shirt and gets the 98 holdout Shirts right: 9.8%.

That looks like a number too obvious to print. On a real belt it is not. If 70% of the items on Corvane's line were one textile (a constructed figure), a model that always answered that textile would score 70% and route every other item wrong. A 72% model would look like a success and be nearly useless. The baseline is the only thing that tells you how much of the score is the model and how much is the mix on the belt, which is why it goes on the plan before the model's own number.

Real world

The 70% that was the belt, not the model (constructed Corvane example). Imagine Corvane's first vendor demo reports 70% accuracy on a week of line footage. Nobody asks what share of that week was the most common textile. If it was 65%, the model adds five points over doing nothing; if it was 30%, it adds forty. The same headline, two completely different products. Ask for the floor in the same sentence as the score, every time.

Part 2 · covers three feature sets at equal labels

Pixels, hand-made features, convolution features

5 min live

Hold the classifier fixed, hold the 300 labels fixed, hold the 1,000 holdout images fixed, and change only what the classifier is shown. Three feature sets, three numbers, computed on the bench's 14 x 14 sample. Then, for scale, the same convolution features with every label in the 2,000-image pool.

LiveWhy the hand-made features lost3 min▶

The hand-made set is 33 measurements a sensible person might write: how much ink sits in each row and each column (the profiles), the total ink, the bounding box of the garment, how symmetric it is left to right. Every one of them is a summary, and every summary throws information away. A row profile knows that row 6 has a lot of ink; it does not know whether that ink is a collar or a pocket. Thirty-three numbers computed from 196 pixels can never hold more than the 196 did.

Raw pixels throw nothing away but know nothing about neighbours. Convolution features keep local shape: an edge here, a corner there, pooled so that a small shift does not matter much. On the bench they win, but by one point: 75.8% against 74.8%. Keep that one point in proportion. The bench's own check of seed luck, rung 4 trained on five different random draws of 300 labels, ranges from 75.6% to 77.6%: a 2.0-point spread. A one-point gap is inside the noise of which labels you happened to buy. The 6.7 points from 300 labels to all 2,000 are not.

Self-studyFor scale: the published table4 min▶

Xiao, Rasul and Vollgraf (2017), the Fashion-MNIST paper, report a benchmark in Table 3: scikit-learn classifiers trained on all 60,000 full-size 28 x 28 training images and scored on the 10,000 test images. LogisticRegression, the same family as tonight's classifier, reaches 0.842. The best entry in the table is an SVC with an RBF kernel at 0.897; KNeighbors 0.854, MLPClassifier 0.871, RandomForest 0.873 and GradientBoosting 0.880 sit between.

Those numbers are not comparable with the bench, and the reason is the whole lesson of controlled comparison. The bench uses 300 labels, not 60,000; 14 x 14 pixels at 16 grey levels, not 28 x 28 at 256; and a 1,000-image holdout. Compare bench rungs with each other, because everything except one thing is held still. Never compare a bench rung with a leaderboard, because almost everything differs. The build-along below sits in between: full-size images, 20,000 labels, and a number close to the paper's.

Real world

When people score below a plain model. The Fashion-MNIST README reports human performance of 0.835: crowd workers labelled 1,000 test images, three labels each, majority vote. The paper's plain LogisticRegression scored 0.842 on the full test set. That does not mean the model sees garments better than people do. It says some of these classes are genuinely hard to tell apart in a 28 x 28 greyscale thumbnail, for anyone. When people and a simple model both struggle on the same classes, more modelling is rarely the cheapest fix; a better camera, or merging two bins, might be.

Part 3 · covers the confusion table and per-class error

The confusion table: where the errors live

5 min live

Accuracy is one number for the whole holdout. A confusion table is a hundred: one row per true class, one column per predicted class, each cell counting how many images of that row's class were called that column's class. The diagonal is the correct answers. Everything off the diagonal is a specific mistake with a specific cost, and on the bench the big ones crowd into a small corner of upper-body garments.

LiveReading the table: rows, columns, one row's error3 min▶

Read a row to ask "when the item really was a Coat, where did it go?" On the bench, 16 Coats went to Shirt and 16 to Pullover. Read a column to ask "when the model said Shirt, what was it really?" Per-class error is one row at a time: the share of that row's images that landed anywhere but the diagonal. At rung 4 that ranges from 41.6% for Pullover and 40.0% for Coat, through 35.7% for Shirt, 30.8% for T-shirt and 30.0% for Dress, down to 10.6% for Trouser and 8.6% for Ankle boot.

The table is not symmetric, and the direction matters. T-shirt read as Shirt is 21; the reverse direction is not among the biggest four. A model can lean one way on a look-alike pair. With all 2,000 labels the leaning changes: the biggest cells become Pullover as Coat 16, Shirt as T-shirt 15 and Coat as Shirt 15, and the worst row is still Shirt at 36.7%. More labels shrank the errors without moving them out of the upper-body corner. Precision, recall and F1, the per-class summaries built from this table, are taught in Intro ML b7: classification metrics; tonight we stay with the raw counts.

Self-studyWhat the table cannot tell you3 min▶

A confusion table counts mistakes; it does not price them. Two cells of 16 can carry very different costs. It also inherits every property of the holdout it was computed on: the bench holdout has 117 T-shirts and 84 Sandals, so a cell's count is partly how many of that class happened to be drawn. Compare rows by error rate, not by raw counts, when the classes are uneven.

And it says nothing about why. T-shirt and Shirt might be confused because the 14 x 14 images lost the buttons and collars (session 1 warned about that), because the classes overlap in the catalogue, or because a few labels disagree with the picture. The table tells you where to look. Pulling up twenty images from the worst cell and looking at them is the next step, every time.

Real world

Two textiles that look alike from above (constructed Corvane example). Picture two fabrics on Corvane's belt that a top-down camera sees as the same shape and grey level, but that belong in different bins because one blends synthetic fibre and the other does not. An overall accuracy of 90% could be made of near-perfect routing on everything else and coin-flip routing on exactly that pair. The bale for the cleaner fibre is the one that gets contaminated. The confusion table is the only page of the plan where that pair shows up by name, which is why it sits next to the accuracy, never behind it.

Build-along

Corvane's baseline and error table, in scikit-learn

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Six steps on the public Fashion-MNIST files with numpy and scikit-learn. This runs on full-size 28 x 28 images with 20,000 training labels, so its numbers are not the bench's 14 x 14, 300-label numbers above and should not match them. Every number printed below is what the code produced; your numbers may differ in the last digit with another library version. The output is page 3 of Corvane's vision plan.

Load the files with the session 1 loader. Same four .gz files in data/, same two functions, plus the class names.

★ Step 1 · loadimport gzip import numpy as np def images(path): with gzip.open(path, "rb") as f: return np.frombuffer(f.read(), np.uint8, offset=16).reshape(-1, 28, 28) def labels(path): with gzip.open(path, "rb") as f: return np.frombuffer(f.read(), np.uint8, offset=8) x_train = images("data/train-images-idx3-ubyte.gz") y_train = labels("data/train-labels-idx1-ubyte.gz") x_test = images("data/t10k-images-idx3-ubyte.gz") y_test = labels("data/t10k-labels-idx1-ubyte.gz") CLASSES = ["T-shirt/top", "Trouser", "Pullover", "Dress", "Coat", "Sandal", "Shirt", "Sneaker", "Bag", "Ankle boot"]

Scale, flatten, and print the floor. Divide by 255 so every pixel sits between 0 and 1, flatten each image to a row of 784, and take a fixed-seed sample of 20,000 training images so the fit stays under a minute. Then the majority baseline: the commonest class in your training labels, scored on all 10,000 test images.

★ Step 2 · scale and baseline# Subsample 20,000 of the 60,000 training images with a fixed seed, # so the fit in step 3 runs in seconds, not minutes. rng = np.random.default_rng(0) idx = rng.choice(len(x_train), size=20000, replace=False) X_tr = x_train[idx].reshape(-1, 784) / 255.0 y_tr = y_train[idx] X_te = x_test.reshape(-1, 784) / 255.0 print(X_tr.shape, X_te.shape, X_tr.max()) # (20000, 784) (10000, 784) 1.0 majority = np.bincount(y_tr).argmax() print(CLASSES[majority], np.bincount(y_tr)[majority]) # Shirt 2043 print((y_test == majority).mean()) # 0.1

Fit softmax regression on raw pixels. scikit-learn's LogisticRegression with ten classes is the same softmax model as Part 1. Give it enough iterations to finish; it converged in 511 here and took about 22 seconds.

★ Step 3 · train and scorefrom sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression clf = LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000) clf.fit(X_tr, y_tr) print(clf.n_iter_) # [511] pred = clf.predict(X_te) print((pred == y_test).mean()) # 0.8312

Build the confusion table and name the three biggest mistakes. Zero the diagonal, then sort the remaining 90 cells. The bench's headline cell, T-shirt read as Shirt, is here too, at 115 of 10,000 test images rather than 21 of 1,000.

★ Step 4 · confusion tablefrom sklearn.metrics import confusion_matrix cm = confusion_matrix(y_test, pred) off = cm.copy() np.fill_diagonal(off, 0) for k in np.argsort(off, axis=None)[::-1][:3]: i, j = divmod(k, 10) print(f"{CLASSES[i]} read as {CLASSES[j]}: {off[i, j]}") # Shirt read as T-shirt/top: 143 # Coat read as Pullover: 123 # Shirt read as Pullover: 122 print(cm[0, 6], cm[2, 4]) # 115 120

Per-class error, worst first. One row of the table at a time. This is the list that goes on the plan, ahead of the overall score.

★ Step 5 · error tableerr = 1 - cm.diagonal() / cm.sum(axis=1) for c in np.argsort(err)[::-1]: print(f"{CLASSES[c]:12s} {err[c]:6.1%}") # Shirt 43.8% · Pullover 27.4% · Coat 26.3% · T-shirt/top 21.4% · Dress 16.1% # Sandal 8.7% · Bag 7.3% · Sneaker 6.7% · Ankle boot 6.5% · Trouser 4.6%

Write page 3 of the plan. Floor, score, worst three, the named pairs, and what a wrong bin costs, saved next to the image card. The cost line is constructed and carries no money figure; Corvane's operations team would fill it in.

★ Step 6 · Corvane's baseline and error table (constructed Corvane example)import json worst = [CLASSES[c] for c in np.argsort(err)[::-1][:3]] pairs = [f"{CLASSES[k // 10]} read as {CLASSES[k % 10]}" for k in np.argsort(off, axis=None)[::-1][:3]] plan = { "page": "3 of 6: baseline classifier and error table", "case": "constructed Corvane example; garments stand in for textiles", "data": "20,000 of 60,000 train images (seed 0), all 10,000 test, 28 x 28", "majority_baseline": round(float((y_test == majority).mean()), 3), "model": "LogisticRegression on 784 raw pixels, scaled 0-1", "model_accuracy": round(float((pred == y_test).mean()), 3), "worst_three": worst, "confused_pairs": pairs, "wrong_bin_cost": "constructed: a look-alike textile in the wrong bin " "contaminates that bale; the error table names which pair", } json.dump(plan, open("vision_plan_p3.json", "w"), indent=2) print(plan["majority_baseline"], plan["model_accuracy"]) # 0.1 0.831 print(plan["worst_three"]) # ['Shirt', 'Pullover', 'Coat'] print(plan["confused_pairs"][0]) # Shirt read as T-shirt/top

Two sets of numbers, never mixed. 83.1% is a 28 x 28, 20,000-label build-along number; 75.8% is a 14 x 14, 300-label bench number; 0.842 is the paper's, on all 60,000. Each answers a different question. The thing that carries across all three is the corner of the table: Shirt, Pullover, Coat and T-shirt hold the biggest errors at every size.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · On the bench, 33 hand-made features scored 69.4% while the 196 raw pixels they were computed from scored 74.8%. Why?

A row profile knows how much ink a row holds, not where in the row it sits. Every summary is a decision about what to discard. Convolution features keep local shape and edged ahead at 75.8%.

2 · The biggest cell off the diagonal at bench rung 4 is T-shirt read as Shirt, 21 of 1,000. What does that mean for Corvane?

The table is not symmetric, and the four biggest cells all trade among T-shirt, Pullover, Coat and Shirt. For Corvane the equivalent is two textiles that look alike from above and belong in different bins (constructed Corvane example).

3 · Why is the majority baseline printed before the model's own score?

On balanced Fashion-MNIST the floor is about 10%, and 9.8% on the bench's 300 labels. On a real line dominated by one textile it could be most of the headline. A score means nothing until you know the floor.
