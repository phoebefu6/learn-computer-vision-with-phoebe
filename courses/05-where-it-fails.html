Where it fails: slices and shift

The best model on the bench scores 82.5% on its holdout, and that number is true. It is also the least useful number you will see tonight. Split it by class and the Shirt is wrong more than a third of the time. Turn the holdout a quarter and the same model gets 5.1% right while reporting 83.7% confidence. Tonight we take one good model and break it on purpose, with array operations that stand in for a lamp, a belt and a camera, so that the failures are found on a page instead of on the line.

Part 1 · covers failure slices

One number hides ten classes and more

4 min live

An accuracy of 82.5% is an average over 1,000 images. Averages are honest about the whole and silent about the parts. A slice is any subset you can name in advance, a class, a lighting condition, a supplier, a size, and the error rate on that subset is the number a sorting line actually lives with. The first slice is always the class. The second is a property of the image itself.

LiveSlice by class, then by a property of the image4 min▶

Read the bars from the top. Shirt is wrong 36.7% of the time, Pullover 32.7%, Coat 31.6%. Those three are the same silhouette at 14 x 14 pixels: an upper-body garment with sleeves. The biggest confusions say so directly: Pullover read as Coat 16 times, Shirt read as T-shirt 15 times, Coat read as Shirt 15 times. At the bottom, Bag and Sandal are wrong about one time in twenty. The overall 82.5% is a blend of a model that is nearly solved on some classes and close to a coin toss between neighbours on others.

The second slice is not a class at all. Sort the 1,000 holdout images by how much ink they carry and take the faintest quarter. The obvious guess is that faint, thin items are harder. On this model they are easier: 215 of 250 correct, 86.0%, against 610 of 750, 81.3%, for the rest. Why they are easier is the next question to measure, not to guess; the point tonight is that the guess was wrong, and a review aimed by that guess would have looked in the wrong place.

Self-studyChoose the slices before you see the results5 min▶

If you cut the holdout a hundred ways after training and report the worst slice, one of them will look alarming by chance alone, especially when a slice holds only a few dozen images. The discipline that prevents it is borrowed from trial design: write the slices down before you score, with the reason each one matters to the business, and report all of them, including the boring ones.

Good slices come from three places. The label set (every class, always). The camera and the site (lamp, belt speed, time of day, the side of the belt an item lands on). The cost of a mistake (the classes that send an item to the wrong bin at the highest cost). For Corvane (constructed Corvane example) that means per class, faint versus heavy items, and anything the camera changes, which is Part 2. A slice with fewer than about a hundred images gets its count printed next to its rate, so nobody reads 3 of 12 as a trend.

Real world

Shirt, Pullover and Coat at Corvane (constructed Corvane example). Suppose Corvane routes cotton tops to one bin and wool outerwear to another. The overall accuracy says the model is fine. The per-class table says that a third of the pullovers go to the wrong bin, and that the confusion is between exactly the two classes the business needs separated. That is not a model problem to tune away; it is a scope question for session 6: do these two bins need a better camera, more labels on that pair, or a person checking that pair only?

Part 2 · covers shift and calibration

The camera changes; the model stays sure

6 min live

A test set drawn from the same pool as the training set measures one thing: how well the model does on more images like the ones it saw. A camera on a belt produces images that are not like that. The lamp dims, items land facing either way, the belt shifts them a few pixels, some arrive turned. Distribution shift is the name for any of these, and the dangerous part is rarely the accuracy drop. It is that the model's confidence does not drop with it.

LiveWhat to click in the widget3 min▶

One model, trained once on all 2,000 pool labels. Each button changes the 1,000 holdout images with one array operation, then scores them again. The shifts are modelled stand-ins for a camera problem; the scoring is real. Click them in order and read two numbers side by side: accuracy and average confidence.

As photographed: 82.5% correct, 82.6% average confidence, ECE 0.022. The model is about as sure as it is right. Lamp dims (x0.6): accuracy barely moves, 81.3%, but confidence falls to 71.7%; the model is now less sure than it should be. Item faces the other way: 56.5% correct at 81.9% confidence, and every Sneaker and every Ankle boot is wrong, Sandal 81.0%. 2 pixels to the right: 36.1% at 64.5%, with Pullover and Shirt 100% wrong. Turned a quarter: 5.1% correct, below the 10% you would get by guessing, at 83.7% confidence, higher than on the clean images.

LiveCalibration and ECE3 min▶

A model is calibrated when its confidence means what it says: of all the images where it reports 80% confidence, about 80% are right. Expected calibration error, ECE, puts one number on the gap. The standard binned definition: sort the predictions into 10 bins by confidence (0 to 0.1, 0.1 to 0.2, and so on), and in each bin take the gap between accuracy and average confidence; ECE is the average of those gaps, weighted by how many images fall in each bin. Zero is honest. The widget's table under the readouts shows the bins themselves.

On clean images this model's ECE is 0.022: nearly honest. Under the quarter turn it is 0.786, because almost every prediction sits in the high-confidence bins and almost none are right. Guo, Pleiss, Sun and Weinberger (2017) report in their abstract that modern neural networks are poorly calibrated, and that temperature scaling, a single parameter fitted after training, is surprisingly effective at fixing it. Note what that fix can and cannot do: it rescales confidence on data like the calibration set. It cannot make a model know that an image is turned. Calibration on the clean holdout says nothing about calibration under shift; you measure both.

For reliability diagrams, Brier score and the full calibration toolkit, Model Evaluation b3, curves and calibration goes into depth. Here we only need the one number and the habit of reading it next to accuracy.

Self-studyCorruption benchmarks: shift as a test suite4 min▶

The five buttons above are a tiny version of an idea that has a published benchmark behind it. Hendrycks and Dietterich (2019) built two benchmarks on ImageNet images: ImageNet-C for common corruptions and ImageNet-P for perturbations, so robustness can be scored as a fixed suite rather than argued about. Their abstract reports "negligible changes in relative corruption robustness from AlexNet classifiers to ResNet classifiers": years of better clean accuracy did not, by their measure, buy proportionally better robustness to corruption.

The lesson for a buyer is procedural. Clean accuracy and robustness are different properties and need different tests. A vendor quoting one number on a clean benchmark has told you nothing about your lamp. Ask for the shift suite, or run your own: the build-along below is five array operations, and each one is a sentence in the scope note about what the camera might do.

Real world

Why the flip breaks the shoes (constructed Corvane example). Fashion-MNIST's photos are catalogue front-looks, and its shoes all face the same way. On a belt, items arrive in any orientation. The model never saw a sneaker pointing the other way, so it read every one as something else: 100% wrong on Sneaker and Ankle boot, 81.0% on Sandal. The fix is not a better classifier; it is to know the camera will produce flipped items and to say so before training, either by collecting them or by flipping on purpose as augmentation, which session 1 called a claim about the camera.

Part 3 · covers detection, segmentation and IoU

Boxes and masks: scoring where, not just what

5 min live

Everything so far answered one question per image: what is it? A belt rarely offers one centred item. Detection answers what and where, as a box around each item. Segmentation answers which pixels, as a mask the shape of the item. Each step up costs more labelling and needs a different score, because a prediction can now be the right class in the wrong place. The standard score for "the right place" is intersection over union, IoU.

LiveClassification, detection, segmentation, and the IoU test4 min▶

Three tasks, three kinds of label. Classification: one class per image, which is all Fashion-MNIST offers. Detection: a box per object, given as two corners, plus a class. Segmentation: a class for every pixel, so the label is a mask. Nothing in this session trains a detector or a segmenter; the point is to know what each one would be scored on before anyone quotes you a number.

A predicted box counts as finding an object when it overlaps the true box enough. The PASCAL VOC Challenge (Everingham et al., 2010) defines the overlap as the area of the intersection divided by the area of the union, and requires that it "must exceed 0.5". The paper says the threshold "was set deliberately low to account for inaccuracies in bounding boxes in the ground truth data": people drawing boxes disagree by a few pixels, and a strict bar would punish the model for the labeller's wobble. VOC also counts duplicate detections of one object as false positives, so a model that draws three boxes around one item is penalised, not rewarded.

COCO's evaluation goes further. Its primary metric averages precision over ten IoU thresholds, .50 to .95 in steps of .05, so loose boxes earn less than tight ones. AP50 is labelled the "PASCAL VOC metric" and AP75 the "strict metric". COCO also reports small objects separately, those under 32 x 32 pixels, which are about 41% of its objects: a reminder that size is a slice too. How these scores hide failures across a vehicle fleet is the subject of Autonomous Driving p5, perception metrics; here we stay with the box.

In the widget, start with a tight guess: IoU 0.876, a hit at both bars. Then slid right by a third: exactly 0.500, a miss at VOC because 0.500 does not exceed 0.5. Too loose: 0.498, a miss, though it contains the whole item. Too small: 0.250, inside the item and still a miss.

Self-studyWhich task does Corvane actually need?3 min▶

Each step up the ladder multiplies the labelling cost: a class per image is one click, a box is two corners per item, a mask is an outline traced around every item. Before choosing, ask what the line does with the answer. If a gate diverts one item at a time from a fixed position under the camera, classification is enough. If several items share a frame and an air jet must hit each one, the jet needs a location, so boxes. Masks earn their cost only when the shape itself drives the action, such as a robot arm choosing where to grip.

For Corvane (constructed Corvane example) the honest answer today is classification, with one line in the plan saying what IoU threshold would apply if boxes are ever needed, and why. Writing it now costs a sentence; arguing about it after a vendor has quoted "92% mAP" without saying which threshold costs a meeting.

Real world

Same metric name, different bar. "mAP" on PASCAL VOC means boxes counted at an overlap above 0.5. On COCO the headline AP averages ten thresholds up to 0.95, which is a much harder test. Two vendors can each report a detection "mAP" and be quoting different metrics. The question to ask is not "what is your mAP?" but "at which IoU threshold, on which object sizes, and on whose images?"

Build-along

Corvane's evaluation slices, from the raw files

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Seven steps in numpy and scikit-learn on the public Fashion-MNIST files. Every number printed below is what the code produced. One honest difference from the widget: the build-along trains a different model, logistic regression on full-size 28 x 28 raw pixels from 10,000 training images, scored on all 10,000 test images. The widget's model is the fixed convolution features on the 14 x 14 bench sample. So the numbers differ; what they share is the shape of the failure. The output is page 5 of Corvane's vision plan (constructed Corvane example).

Load the four files. The same loader as session 1, reading the .gz files from your data/ folder.

★ Step 1 · loadimport gzip import numpy as np def images(path): with gzip.open(path, "rb") as f: return np.frombuffer(f.read(), np.uint8, offset=16).reshape(-1, 28, 28) def labels(path): with gzip.open(path, "rb") as f: return np.frombuffer(f.read(), np.uint8, offset=8) x_train = images("data/train-images-idx3-ubyte.gz") y_train = labels("data/train-labels-idx1-ubyte.gz") x_test = images("data/t10k-images-idx3-ubyte.gz") y_test = labels("data/t10k-labels-idx1-ubyte.gz") CLASSES = ["T-shirt/top", "Trouser", "Pullover", "Dress", "Coat", "Sandal", "Shirt", "Sneaker", "Bag", "Ankle boot"] print(x_train.shape, x_test.shape) # (60000, 28, 28) (10000, 28, 28)

Train one model and leave it alone. Logistic regression on raw pixels scaled to 0 to 1, fitted on 10,000 training images drawn with a fixed seed so the fit stays under a minute. It reaches 82.4% on the 10,000 test images. For scale, the Fashion-MNIST paper reports 0.842 for logistic regression on all 60,000.

★ Step 2 · one modelfrom sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression def flat(imgs): return imgs.reshape(len(imgs), -1) / 255.0 rng = np.random.default_rng(5) # fixed seed: same 10,000 every run idx = rng.choice(len(x_train), 10000, replace=False) # subsample keeps the fit under a minute model = LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000) model.fit(flat(x_train[idx]), y_train[idx]) pred = model.predict(flat(x_test)) print(f"clean accuracy {(pred == y_test).mean():.3f}") # clean accuracy 0.824

Slice it. Per-class error, worst first, then the faint-item slice. Same shape as the widget: Shirt, Pullover and Coat at the top, and the faintest quarter scoring better than the rest, 86.3% (2,157 of 2,500) against 81.1% (6,086 of 7,500).

★ Step 3 · slicesfor c in np.argsort([-(pred[y_test == c] != c).mean() for c in range(10)]): print(f"{CLASSES[c]:12s} {(pred[y_test == c] != c).mean():6.1%} wrong") # Shirt 45.9% wrong # Pullover 28.0% wrong # Coat 25.8% wrong # T-shirt/top 21.0% wrong # Dress 16.9% wrong # Sandal 9.0% wrong # Sneaker 8.9% wrong # Bag 8.0% wrong # Ankle boot 7.1% wrong # Trouser 5.1% wrong ink = x_test.reshape(len(x_test), -1).mean(axis=1) faint = ink <= np.quantile(ink, 0.25) # the faintest quarter by mean ink print("faint", (pred[faint] == y_test[faint]).sum(), "of", faint.sum(), "| rest", (pred[~faint] == y_test[~faint]).sum(), "of", (~faint).sum()) # faint 2157 of 2500 | rest 6086 of 7500

Change the camera. Five versions of the full-size test set, each one array operation, scored by the same model. The quarter turn drops to 3.1% correct at 86.2% average confidence; the flip sends every Ankle boot wrong.

★ Step 4 · shiftsshifts = { "as photographed": x_test, "lamp dims (x0.6)": (x_test * 0.6).astype(np.uint8), "faces the other way": np.flip(x_test, axis=2), # np.fliplr on every image "2 pixels right": np.roll(x_test, 2, axis=2), # wraps 2 edge columns, nearly all 0 "turned a quarter": np.rot90(x_test, 1, axes=(1, 2)), } probs = {} for name, imgs in shifts.items(): p = model.predict_proba(flat(imgs)) probs[name] = p print(f"{name:20s} accuracy {(p.argmax(1) == y_test).mean():6.1%} confidence {p.max(1).mean():6.1%}") # as photographed accuracy 82.4% confidence 86.9% # lamp dims (x0.6) accuracy 77.2% confidence 76.6% # faces the other way accuracy 55.7% confidence 81.2% # 2 pixels right accuracy 45.8% confidence 74.5% # turned a quarter accuracy 3.1% confidence 86.2% flipped = probs["faces the other way"].argmax(1) for c in (5, 7, 9): print(f"flipped {CLASSES[c]:11s} {(flipped[y_test == c] != c).mean():6.1%} wrong") # flipped Sandal 67.8% wrong # flipped Sneaker 88.6% wrong # flipped Ankle boot 100.0% wrong

Write ECE yourself. Ten bins by confidence, the gap between accuracy and confidence in each, weighted by the share of images in the bin. Clean, 0.045; turned a quarter, 0.831.

★ Step 5 · ECEdef ece(probs, y, bins=10): conf = probs.max(axis=1) right = probs.argmax(axis=1) == y edges = np.linspace(0, 1, bins + 1) total = 0.0 for lo, hi in zip(edges[:-1], edges[1:]): inside = (conf > lo) & (conf <= hi) if inside.any(): total += inside.mean() * abs(right[inside].mean() - conf[inside].mean()) return total print(f"ECE as photographed {ece(probs['as photographed'], y_test):.3f}") # 0.045 print(f"ECE turned a quarter {ece(probs['turned a quarter'], y_test):.3f}") # 0.831

Write IoU and check it against the widget. Boxes as (x0, y0, x1, y1). The four presets reproduce the widget exactly, because this part is pure geometry with no model in it. Note the strict >: VOC says the overlap must exceed 0.5.

★ Step 6 · IoUdef iou(a, b): ix = max(0, min(a[2], b[2]) - max(a[0], b[0])) iy = max(0, min(a[3], b[3]) - max(a[1], b[1])) inter = ix * iy union = (a[2] - a[0]) * (a[3] - a[1]) + (b[2] - b[0]) * (b[3] - b[1]) - inter return inter / union truth = (40, 30, 160, 150) guesses = {"a tight guess": (46, 34, 164, 152), "slid right by a third": (80, 30, 200, 150), "too loose": (20, 10, 190, 180), "too small": (70, 60, 130, 120)} for name, box in guesses.items(): v = iou(truth, box) print(f"{name:22s} IoU {v:.3f} VOC hit: {v > 0.5}") # a tight guess IoU 0.876 VOC hit: True # slid right by a third IoU 0.500 VOC hit: False # too loose IoU 0.498 VOC hit: False # too small IoU 0.250 VOC hit: False

Run the shifts before the model is good, not after. A shift suite takes five lines and a minute. Run it on the first baseline, and every model after that is compared on clean accuracy and on the suite. A model that gains two points clean and loses ten on the quarter turn is a worse model for a belt.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 5 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Under "turned a quarter" the model gets 5.1% right while reporting 83.7% average confidence. Why is that the most dangerous row of the shift table?

The dimmed lamp lowers confidence, so a rule that routes low-confidence items to a person would catch it. The quarter turn raises confidence while accuracy collapses, ECE 0.786, so the same rule waves the mistakes through. Low accuracy you can see is a nuisance; low accuracy at high confidence is a hidden failure.

2 · A predicted box slid right by a third of its width scores IoU 0.500 against the true box. Under PASCAL VOC, is it a hit?

Everingham et al. (2010) state the overlap "must exceed 0.5". The comparison is strict, which is why the build-along writes v > 0.5. Too loose at 0.498 is also a miss, though it contains the whole item.

3 · Before measuring, a teammate is sure the faintest items will be the hardest. The faint slice scores 86.0% (215 of 250) against 81.3% for the rest. What does that teach?

The build-along finds the same direction on a different model: 86.3% for the faint quarter against 81.1%. The overall 82.5% is not wrong; it is an average, and slices show what it averages. Choose slices in advance, measure all of them, report the counts.
