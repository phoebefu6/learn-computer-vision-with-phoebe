Learn Computer Vision Foundations with Phoebe · Session 1 of 6

An image is a grid of numbers

A camera does not hand a model a picture. It hands over a grid of numbers, one per pixel per channel, and everything a vision model ever does is arithmetic on that grid. Once you can see the grid, most of the strange behaviour of vision systems stops being strange: why a brighter photo can come out darker, why a flipped shoe confuses a classifier, why a model that scores well in the lab fails under a new lamp. Tonight we open real images, read their numbers, and change them with array operations you can check by hand.

Part 1 · covers pixels, resolution, what the model receives

A picture arrives as 784 numbers

6 min live

Take one real test image from Fashion-MNIST, a T-shirt. It is 28 pixels tall and 28 wide, one value per pixel, each value an integer from 0 to 255. That is 784 numbers. A model that takes raw pixels receives exactly those 784 numbers in a row, and nothing else: no label, no outline, no idea which number sat next to which.

LiveWhat one pixel value means, here and in your camera3 min▶

Each pixel is an 8-bit integer, 0 to 255, stored as uint8. In an ordinary photo, 0 is black and 255 is white. Fashion-MNIST is the other way round: the dataset's conversion pipeline negated the intensities after shrinking each catalogue photo, so the background reads 0 and the garment reads high. The T-shirt in the figure has 303 of its 784 pixels at exactly 0 and one pixel at 255.

This matters more than it looks. "Dark" and "bright" are conventions written into a pipeline, and a model trained on one convention reads the other as a different object. Corvane's camera will produce ordinary photos, light belt and darker items, or the reverse under a different lamp. The first line of any image card is what 0 means.

Self-studyResolution: what shrinking keeps and what it throws away4 min▶

The Fashion-MNIST photos started as catalogue thumbnails and were trimmed, resized so the longest edge is 28 pixels, sharpened, centred, negated and converted to 8-bit greyscale. Every one of those steps is a decision someone made, and the published paper lists them in order, which is exactly what a good image card does.

The bench in session 4 goes one step further to fit 3,000 images into a web page: it averages each 2 x 2 block into one pixel (14 x 14, 196 numbers) and keeps 16 grey levels instead of 256. A T-shirt is still a T-shirt at that size; a shirt's buttons are gone. Whether that loss matters depends on the question. For "which bin", maybe not. For "is this shirt torn", certainly. Resolution is a budget decision, the same kind as the label budget, and it should be written down with its reason.

Real world

Why Fashion-MNIST exists at all. Its authors at Zalando Research built it in 2017 as a drop-in replacement for MNIST, the handwritten digits, with the same image size, format and 60,000 / 10,000 split. Their README gives the reasons bluntly: "MNIST is too easy", convolutional nets reach 99.7% on it, and it is overused. A benchmark nobody can fail stops telling you anything. The same is true of a test set at a plant: if every model passes it, it is measuring the test set, not the models.

Part 2 · covers channels and array shape

Colour is three grids stacked

4 min live

Fashion-MNIST has one channel, so each image is a single 28 x 28 grid. A colour camera stores three grids of the same size, one each for red, green and blue, and one pixel is the three values at the same position. Nothing else changes: still integers, still 0 to 255, just three times as many of them.

LiveShape conventions: which axis is the channel3 min▶

A colour image is a three-axis array, and libraries disagree about the order. numpy and most image readers give (height, width, channels); PyTorch layers expect (channels, height, width) and a batch axis in front of that. Fashion-MNIST, with one channel, is usually stored as (n, 28, 28) with the channel axis left out entirely.

Getting the order wrong rarely raises an error. A 720 x 1280 x 3 array read as 3 x 720 x 1280 is still a valid array; the model simply sees stripes. The habit that prevents it: print .shape and .dtype at every boundary between libraries, and write the expected shape into the image card.

Self-studyGreyscale is a choice, not a loss-free default3 min▶

Converting colour to greyscale replaces three numbers with one weighted sum. Common conventions weight green most because the eye is most sensitive to it; check the weights your library uses rather than assuming. Fewer numbers means a smaller model and less data needed, and for shape questions (is this a shoe or a shirt) it often costs little.

For Corvane it might cost a lot. Two textiles of the same shape and different fibre can differ only in colour, and greyscale makes them identical. The question "does the bin depend on colour?" belongs in the scope note before anyone decides the camera, not after the first model disappoints.

Part 3 · covers brightness, contrast, flips as array operations

Brightness, contrast and flips are arithmetic

5 min live

If an image is a grid of numbers, then making it brighter is adding a number, more contrast is stretching around the middle, and a mirror image is reading each row backwards. Each of these is one line of numpy, and each has a way to go quietly wrong.

LiveTry it on real images4 min▶

The widget below holds 20 real Fashion-MNIST test images at full size. Pick one, apply an operation, and read the numbers the model would receive. The table shows the middle 8 x 8 block; the readouts are computed over all 784 pixels, in your browser.

Start with the T-shirt. As stored: mean 36.6, 303 background pixels, 1 at 255. Press brighter (+60): the mean goes to 96.4, the background count goes to 0 because every 0 became 60, and 4 pixels are now clipped at 255. Then pick the Coat and brighten it: 475 of its 784 pixels hit the ceiling, because a coat is mostly ink to begin with. The same operation destroys more information in some classes than in others.

Then press flip left-right. The mean does not move. Every summary statistic is the same. And yet the 784 numbers in the row are now in a different order, and a model that reads pixels by position sees a different input. Session 5 measures exactly what that does to shoes.

Real world

The augmentation that made things worse. A common recipe for "more training data" is to add flipped, brightened and rotated copies of every image. It works when the change is one the real camera will also produce. It quietly teaches the wrong thing when it is not: a recycling camera mounted above a belt sees items from one side, under one lamp, and flipping text-bearing packaging or brightening already-saturated white plastic adds images the line will never produce. Every augmentation is a claim about the camera, and belongs in the image card.

Build-along

Corvane's image card, from the raw files

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Six steps in plain numpy on the public Fashion-MNIST files: no deep learning library, nothing hidden. Every number printed below is what the code produced on the real files. The output is the first page of Corvane's vision plan: an image card.

Get the four files and read them with numpy. Download the four .gz files from the data/fashion folder of the Fashion-MNIST repository into an empty data/ folder. The format is tiny: a 16-byte header for images, 8 bytes for labels, then raw bytes.

★ Step 1 · loadimport gzip import numpy as np def images(path): with gzip.open(path, "rb") as f: return np.frombuffer(f.read(), np.uint8, offset=16).reshape(-1, 28, 28) def labels(path): with gzip.open(path, "rb") as f: return np.frombuffer(f.read(), np.uint8, offset=8) x_train = images("data/train-images-idx3-ubyte.gz") y_train = labels("data/train-labels-idx1-ubyte.gz") x_test = images("data/t10k-images-idx3-ubyte.gz") y_test = labels("data/t10k-labels-idx1-ubyte.gz") print(x_train.shape, x_train.dtype, x_test.shape) # (60000, 28, 28) uint8 (10000, 28, 28) print(x_train.min(), x_train.max()) # 0 255

Look at one image as numbers. Test image 164 is the T-shirt from the widget. Print a 4 x 4 window and the counts.

★ Step 2 · one imageCLASSES = ["T-shirt/top", "Trouser", "Pullover", "Dress", "Coat", "Sandal", "Shirt", "Sneaker", "Bag", "Ankle boot"] img = x_test[164] print(CLASSES[y_test[164]], img.shape) # T-shirt/top (28, 28) print(img[10:14, 12:16]) # [[ 51 51 57 51] # [ 57 48 48 57] # [ 43 45 27 34] # [107 141 73 107]] print(img.mean().round(2), (img == 0).sum(), (img == 255).sum()) # 36.58 303 1

Brighten it twice, the wrong way and the right way. In uint8, 200 + 60 wraps to 4. Count how many pixels got darker when you meant to brighten.

★ Step 3 · the uint8 trapwrong = img + 60 # stays uint8: modulo 256 print((wrong < img).sum()) # 4 pixels went DARK right = np.clip(img.astype(np.int16) + 60, 0, 255).astype(np.uint8) print(right.mean().round(2), (right == 0).sum(), (right == 255).sum()) # 96.36 0 4 flipped = img[:, ::-1] print(flipped.mean().round(2), (flipped != img).sum()) # 36.58 518

Check the balance. Fashion-MNIST was built balanced on purpose. A real line never is, and session 3 shows what imbalance does to a majority-class baseline.

★ Step 4 · class countsprint(np.bincount(y_train)) # [6000 6000 6000 6000 6000 6000 6000 6000 6000 6000] print(np.bincount(y_test)) # [1000 1000 1000 1000 1000 1000 1000 1000 1000 1000]

Measure ink per class. The mean pixel value per class is a crude first feature, and it already separates some classes: sandals are mostly gaps, coats are mostly cloth.

★ Step 5 · ink by classink = x_train.reshape(len(x_train), -1).mean(axis=1) for c in range(10): print(f"{CLASSES[c]:12s} {ink[y_train == c].mean():6.1f}") # T-shirt/top 83.0 · Trouser 56.8 · Pullover 96.1 · Dress 66.0 · Coat 98.3 # Sandal 34.9 · Shirt 84.6 · Sneaker 42.8 · Bag 90.2 · Ankle boot 76.8

Write the card. A dictionary saved next to the data, in the same commit. The last field is the one that matters most and gets skipped most.

★ Step 6 · Corvane's image card (constructed Corvane example)import json card = { "source": "Fashion-MNIST, Xiao, Rasul, Vollgraf 2017, MIT licence", "shape": "(n, 28, 28) uint8, one channel", "zero_means": "background; high values are ink (intensities negated)", "balance": "6,000 per class train, 1,000 per class test", "ink_by_class": "Sandal 34.9 lowest, Coat 98.3 highest", "augmentations_allowed": "none yet; each one is a claim about the camera", "is_not": "not waste, not on a belt, not under Corvane's lamp; stand-in only", } json.dump(card, open("image_card.json", "w"), indent=2)

Keep the card next to the data, not in a slide. Session 3 adds the baseline and its error table to the same plan, session 4 the labelling budget, session 5 the failure slices, session 6 the signed scope note. Six pages, one file, one owner.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · You brighten a uint8 image with img + 60 and four of the brightest pixels come out nearly black. What happened?

Fixed-width integer arithmetic wraps silently: no error, no warning. The fix is three operations, widen, add, clip, and on the T-shirt it turns 4 dark pixels into 4 clipped white ones.

2 · In Fashion-MNIST the background of every image reads 0 and the garment reads high. Why, and why write it down?

The Fashion-MNIST paper lists the negation as one step of seven. "What 0 means" is a pipeline decision, which is why it is the first field on the image card and not an assumption.

3 · Flipping the T-shirt leaves its mean at 36.58, yet 518 of its 784 pixel values change position. What does that tell you about a raw-pixel model?

A model that reads pixels by position has no idea that the flipped row is the same object. Whether that matters is measured, not assumed: session 5 flips the holdout and watches shoes fail.
